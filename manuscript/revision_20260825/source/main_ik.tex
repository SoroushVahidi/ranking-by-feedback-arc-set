\begin{abstract}
Deriving global rankings from noisy and incomplete pairwise comparisons is a fundamental problem with applications in sports analytics, preference aggregation, and evaluation. Many recent approaches rely on learning-based models, but in practice ranking systems are often required to operate under strict constraints on training cost, runtime, and reproducibility. We study a scalable, training-free alternative that constructs rankings directly from weighted directed comparison graphs.

Our approach represents pairwise comparisons as a weighted digraph and leverages the connection between ranking inconsistency and feedback-arc-set removal to build an acyclic comparison backbone. The proposed pipeline is time-bounded and consists of three stages: a local-ratio-style cycle-breaking heuristic, a stable weight-prioritized add-back procedure with up to three passes while preserving acyclicity, and an optional bounded score-refinement stage under upset-based objectives used in recent ranking benchmarks. The resulting method outputs a real-valued score vector whose induced order is consistent with the recovered acyclic structure.

Across the benchmark suite used in recent ranking-from-comparisons work, the proposed method is competitive with strong classical baselines and delivers substantial runtime advantages over training-based GNNRank configurations under fixed wall-clock budgets. These results position the method as a practical, deterministic, and scalable alternative for ranking on large pairwise-comparison graphs when training cost and deployment efficiency are important.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Ranking from pairwise comparisons arises throughout statistics, machine learning, and network analysis, from sports analytics to preference aggregation. Methods typically return a real-valued score vector whose induced order is evaluated by upset-based losses \citet{he22}. Classical estimators are fast and training-free; learning-based directed GNNs such as GNNRank can be accurate but introduce training cost, hyperparameter sensitivity, and reduced reproducibility.

Directed comparison graphs almost always contain cycles arising from noise, incompleteness, and local inconsistencies. A combinatorial response is to delete a set of directed arcs so that the remaining graph is acyclic, then read a ranking from a topological order of the resulting directed acyclic graph (DAG). This is the Minimum Weighted Feedback Arc Set (MWFAS) viewpoint: minimize the total weight of deleted comparisons subject to acyclicity. Related spectral and synchronization methods already note connections between ranking inconsistency and feedback structure \citet{SerialRank,SyncRank}. MWFAS is NP-hard \citet{K72}, so exact minimization is generally impractical, yet the formulation supplies a precise consistency-restoration objective for ranking.

We emphasize at the outset that this paper does \emph{not} propose a new general approximation algorithm for MWFAS. Our objective is a practical, training-free, deterministic, and time-bounded ranking pipeline for weighted pairwise-comparison graphs. Prior work by \citet{VK25} already operationalized ranking via the local-ratio cycle-breaking heuristic of Demetrescu and Finocchi \citet{CI03}, together with weight-prioritized reinsertion of deleted arcs under an exact cycle-safety test and optional order-preserving score refinement. The present manuscript clarifies that lineage, corrects an implementation-fidelity gap, adds theory and a secondary structural repair, and expands empirical validation.

To make the ranking--MWFAS connection precise, we begin with formal definitions.

\begin{definition}[Ranking Problem]
Let $G=(V,E)$ be a directed graph, where $V$ is the set of items and $E$ is the set of directed pairwise-comparison edges.
Each edge $(i,j)\in E$ has weight $w_{ij}>0$, representing the strength of the preference for $i$ over $j$.
A \emph{ranking} is a total order on $V$, represented equivalently by a bijection $R:V\rightarrow\{1,\dots,|V|\}$, where smaller values indicate higher rank.
An edge $(i,j)$ is \emph{inconsistent} with $R$ if $R(i)>R(j)$.
The goal is to find a ranking that minimizes the total weight of inconsistent edges:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ranking_obj}
\min_{R}\ \sum_{(i,j)\in E:\ R(i)>R(j)} w_{ij}.
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Minimum Weighted Feedback Arc Set (MWFAS)]
Let $G=(V,E)$ be a directed graph with edge weights $w_{ij}>0$ for each $(i,j)\in E$.
A set of edges $F\subseteq E$ is a \emph{feedback arc set} if removing $F$ makes the graph acyclic, i.e., $(V,E\setminus F)$ is a directed acyclic graph (DAG).
The MWFAS problem is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mwfas}
\min_{F\subseteq E}\ \sum_{(i,j)\in F} w_{ij}
\quad \text{s.t.} \quad (V,E\setminus F)\ \text{is acyclic.}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

The ranking objective~\eqref{eq:ranking_obj} and MWFAS~\eqref{eq:mwfas} share the same optimum \emph{value}: the backward edges of any ranking form a feedback arc set of equal weight, and any feedback arc set induces a DAG whose topological orders realize that deleted weight as ranking disagreement. The correspondence between optimal rankings and optimal feedback arc sets is many-to-many rather than one-to-one; we treat this equivalence as a formal claim to be stated and proved, not merely asserted.

\subsection{Scope, lineage, and contributions}
The pipeline studied here (\S\ref{sec:framework}) builds an acyclic backbone with the local-ratio heuristic of \citet{CI03}, reinserts high-weight arcs while preserving acyclicity, extracts scores from the resulting DAG, and optionally applies order-preserving refinement. Several components are \emph{prior}: local-ratio Phase~A, exact cycle-safe add-back (including weight ordering), and ternary/refinement ideas already appear in \citet{CI03,VK25}. What this paper contributes is therefore not a reinvention of that core, but the following.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Formalization and guarantee boundaries.}
We prove ranking--MWFAS optimum-value equivalence under the many-to-many correspondence above, restate implementation complexity carefully, and audit when the Demetrescu--Finocchi guarantee does and does not transfer. In particular, a practical timeout-bounded run does \emph{not} automatically inherit a nontrivial general approximation guarantee under early termination.
\item \textbf{Implementation-fidelity correction.}
The submitted codebase used a weaker fixed-topological-position proxy in place of the intended exact reachability test for cycle-safe reinsertion. Restoring exact reachability is fidelity to the established specification, not a newly invented mechanism; controlled ablations show that the correction materially improves ranking quality relative to the proxy.
\item \textbf{Secondary structural repair.}
We introduce a conflict-triggered weighted min-cut exchange with proofs of acyclicity preservation, strict improvement of the weighted feedback objective, and finite termination under strict improvement. Empirically, its benefit is regime-specific rather than universal.
\item \textbf{Expanded empirical validation.}
We compare against classical and GNNRank baselines under a common protocol with timeout-aware coverage, paired statistics, family-aware sensitivity (addressing Basketball-dominated suites), and a full ablation/sensitivity/scalability campaign. The modest expansion of the dataset suite relative to prior work is not presented as a primary novelty.
\end{enumerate}

Empirically, ranking quality shows strong gains against several classical and GNN methods, while remaining competitive---not uniformly superior---with \textsc{SpringRank}, \textsc{DavidScore}, and \textsc{SVD\_NRS} under family-aware analysis; \textsc{BTL} remains stronger on \texttt{upset\_ratio}. On runtime, the pipeline is slower than lightweight classical baselines and substantially faster than trained GNNRank models under the evaluated end-to-end training protocol. Scalability is strong on the evaluated sparse and moderately dense instances; the near-complete Finance graph is an explicit large-dense boundary case.

\subsection{Related Works}

\subsubsection{Previous Work on Ranking from Pairwise Comparisons}
Ranking from noisy and incomplete pairwise comparisons has been studied through statistical, spectral, random-walk, and optimization-based formulations \citet{H00,CGZ21,SW16}. Since our experiments evaluate score-based outputs under upset-style losses, we focus here on the principal baseline families most relevant to our benchmark and to the positioning of our method as a training-free ranking pipeline.

A first major family is probabilistic and statistical ranking. \textsc{BTL} (Bradley--Terry) models each item with a positive latent ability parameter and defines pairwise win probabilities as $\Pr(i \succ j)=\pi_i/(\pi_i+\pi_j)$ \citet{BTL}. Estimating these abilities yields real-valued scores that induce a global ranking and remains one of the standard baselines for paired-comparison data. \textsc{DavidScore} provides a simpler summary-based alternative that aggregates possibly unbalanced paired-comparison outcomes into a single score per item \citet{DavidScore}.

A second family uses random-walk and spectral constructions. \textsc{RankCentrality} builds a Markov chain from pairwise outcomes and uses its stationary distribution as a ranking score vector \citet{NS17}. \textsc{SerialRank} constructs a similarity/seriation matrix and then applies spectral seriation to recover an ordering \citet{SerialRank}, while \textsc{SyncRank} formulates ranking from incomplete and noisy comparisons as an angular synchronization problem and recovers scores through spectral or SDP-based relaxations \citet{SyncRank}. Related low-rank approaches include \textsc{SVD\_RS} and \textsc{SVD\_NRS}, which derive one-dimensional ranking scores from pairwise measurements using singular-vector structure under different normalization choices \citet{SVD_RS_NRS}.

A third group consists of graph-based scoring methods that operate directly on the directed comparison network. \textsc{EigenvectorCentrality} assigns scores recursively through weighted endorsements from neighboring nodes \citet{EigenvectorCentrality}, while \textsc{PageRank} interprets importance through the stationary distribution of a random walk with teleportation \citet{PageRank}. \textsc{SpringRank} provides a physics-inspired alternative by embedding nodes on a real-valued axis through minimization of a quadratic spring-energy objective over directed edges, leading to a scalable linear-system-based estimator \citet{SpringRank}.

More recently, learning-based methods have used graph neural networks to infer ranking scores from directed comparison graphs. \textsc{GNNRank} learns global rankings from pairwise comparisons using directed-GNN backbones and differentiable objectives tailored to ranking losses \citet{he22}. In our experiments we use the two backbones provided in that framework: \textsc{DIGRAC}, which is designed to capture directionality and flow imbalance in directed graphs \citet{DIGRAC}, and an inception-style directed convolutional backbone (\textsc{ib}) that combines multi-hop proximity channels for directed graphs \citet{DiGCN}. These learning-based methods provide a useful point of comparison because they often achieve strong ranking quality but incur training cost and additional hyperparameter sensitivity.

Our work differs from the above families in emphasis rather than problem setting. Instead of learning ranking scores through statistical estimation or graph-neural training, we exploit combinatorial acyclicity structure to construct a deterministic, training-free ranking pipeline that is evaluated under the same upset-based criteria as these baselines.

\subsubsection{Previous Work About Minimum Weighted Feedback Arc Set}
The Minimum Weighted Feedback Arc Set (MWFAS) problem has been studied extensively from approximation, heuristic, exact, and parameterized perspectives \citet{K72,FPP20}. In general terms, MWFAS asks for a minimum-weight set of directed edges whose removal makes a graph acyclic, and it is a natural combinatorial formulation for resolving inconsistencies in directed comparison data.

Early approximation work by \citet{EG95} was strengthened by \citet{EG2000}, who developed divide-and-conquer methods based on spreading metrics and linear-programming relaxations. While these results are theoretically important, their reliance on comparatively heavy optimization machinery limits their practical appeal for large ranking graphs. For faster practical computation, \citet{CI03} proposed an $O(|V||E|)$ heuristic based on the local-ratio framework of \citet{BK04}, together with an approximation guarantee parameterized by the length of the longest directed cycle. This line is particularly relevant to our work because it provides the algorithmic starting point for our cycle-breaking stage.

Exact and near-exact approaches have also been studied, primarily for smaller or sparser instances. \citet{BA21} presented an exact method for the unweighted minimum feedback arc set problem on sparse directed graphs using lazy cycle enumeration, while \citet{HM21} studied tight localizations of feedback sets and algorithms whose worst-case running times can become prohibitive on dense graphs. These approaches are valuable as methodological reference points, but their scalability is limited in the large benchmark settings targeted in our paper.

From a parameterized viewpoint, \citet{RS06} investigated feedback set problems in tournaments, including weighted and unweighted feedback arc set variants, and obtained fixed-parameter algorithms with running times exponential in the solution size. Related approximation work by \citet{HR94} considered the maximum acyclic subgraph problem, which is closely connected to unweighted feedback arc deletion. More recently, \citet{SJ21} explored satisfiability-modulo-theories approaches to MFAS using incremental solving, again highlighting the trade-off between solution quality and scalability.

Recent heuristic work has also emphasized structural refinements of feedback-arc-set solutions. \citet{c24} introduced the notions of minimal and stable feedback arc sets and proposed the MS-FAS heuristic based on strongly connected component decomposition and iterative refinement. Building on this direction, \citet{c25} examined how different initial orderings, including centrality-based ones, affect MS-FAS behavior and reported robustness to initialization across benchmark instances.

As stated above, we do not claim a new general MWFAS approximation algorithm. The Related Work above situates the classical and learning-based ranking baselines and the MWFAS literature that frames our pipeline; the contribution list in the preceding subsection delimits prior components from the theory, fidelity, min-cut, and empirical additions developed in this revision.

\section{A Framework for Ranking from Pairwise Comparisons via MWFAS}
\label{sec:framework}

We adopt the local-ratio feedback-arc-set heuristic of Demetrescu and Finocchi~\cite{CI03} as the combinatorial core and develop it into a deterministic, time-bounded ranking pipeline for weighted pairwise-comparison graphs. Given a directed comparison graph, the proposed pipeline (i) constructs an acyclic backbone through cycle-based residual reduction and arc removal (Phase~1), (ii) selectively reinserts high-weight arcs while preserving acyclicity (Phase~2), (iii) extracts a ranking from the resulting DAG and assigns real-valued scores consistent with that order, and (iv) optionally refines score magnitudes under bounded computation while preserving the induced ranking (Phase~C). The underlying local-ratio heuristic of~\citet{CI03} comes with an approximation guarantee bounded by the length of a longest simple directed cycle in the input graph. 

We do not claim any new approximation guarantee beyond that underlying result. Rather, our contribution is a practical, scalable, and training-free ranking-oriented instantiation of the MWFAS viewpoint, with deterministic tie-breaking, explicit time budgeting, robust numerical handling, multi-pass add-back variants, and an optional order-preserving refinement stage. In this sense, the method is intended not as an exact solver for MWFAS, but as a deployable alternative to learning-based ranking methods when runtime, reproducibility, and training cost are important considerations.

\subsection{Problem Setting and Input Representation}
\label{subsec:framework_problem}

\paragraph{Comparison graph.}
Let $V=\{1,\dots,n\}$ denote the items to be ranked. We represent pairwise comparisons by a weighted directed graph $G=(V,E,w)$, encoded as a nonnegative weighted adjacency matrix $A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$, where $A_{ij}$ denotes the aggregated weight of the directed comparison $i\to j$. The graph is fully general and is not restricted to tournaments; in particular, both $A_{ij}>0$ and $A_{ji}>0$ may occur for the same pair of items. In the implementation, only strictly positive weights are retained, so entries with $A_{ij}\le 0$ are ignored in the internal representation.

\paragraph{Input representation.}
The canonical solver input is a sparse adjacency matrix. In the benchmark pipeline, datasets are loaded as sparse adjacency files and stored as \texttt{scipy.sparse.csr\_matrix} objects, which are then passed directly to the proposed method. For completeness, the implementation also accepts dense inputs and converts them to CSR format before invoking the core routine.

\paragraph{Internal edge-list form.}
For computation, the sparse matrix $A$ is converted to an edge-list representation with arrays
\texttt{src}, \texttt{dst}, and \texttt{w} of length $m$, where each strictly positive entry of $A$ contributes one directed edge. Thus, antiparallel comparisons are preserved as distinct arcs whenever both directions are present. Unless explicitly stated otherwise, edge weights are used in their original form and are not normalized or rescaled before the acyclicity-enforcement stages.

\paragraph{Outputs and score convention.}
The core routine returns a real-valued score vector $s\in\mathbb{R}^n$, where larger values indicate higher rank. When a discrete ordering is needed for evaluation, the benchmark derives it from the score vector in a deterministic way. The implementation can also return auxiliary metadata, including runtime and per-phase diagnostics such as the number of edges removed in Phase~1, the number reinserted in each add-back pass, and the corresponding phase timings.

\paragraph{Determinism and tie-breaking.}
A central design goal of the pipeline is deterministic behavior under fixed input and time budget. In Phases~1 and~2, cycle search traverses vertices in increasing index order and explores outgoing edges in stored edge order, while the add-back stage processes candidate edges in a stable descending-weight order. Topological ordering for add-back and score extraction is computed with Kahn's algorithm under a deterministic queue policy: initially eligible zero-indegree vertices are processed in increasing index order, and newly eligible vertices are appended as they arise. The optional score-refinement stage preserves the induced order but can introduce minor non-determinism when exact score ties are present and tie-breaking depends on non-stable sorting. Aside from such score-tie effects, the pipeline is deterministic for fixed inputs and time limits.

\subsection{Phase 1: Local-Ratio Cycle Breaking (Acyclic Backbone)}
\label{subsec:framework_phase1}

This subsection describes Phase~1 of our pipeline, which produces an \emph{acyclic backbone} by iteratively identifying directed cycles and removing a subset of arcs.
The procedure is a local-ratio-style heuristic: it maintains residual arc weights and repeatedly reduces residual mass along a detected cycle, deleting arcs whose residual reaches (near) zero.
We emphasize that Phase~1 is a practical heuristic component; we do not claim a new approximation guarantee beyond what is known for local-ratio-type feedback-arc-set heuristics, and our contribution is the ranking-oriented, time-bounded, deterministic instantiation used in the experimental protocol.

\paragraph{State and initialization.}
Given the input adjacency $A$, we first form an edge list $(\texttt{src},\texttt{dst},\texttt{w})$ containing one directed edge for each strictly positive entry of $A$ (see \S\ref{subsec:framework_problem}).
Phase~1 maintains:
(i) a residual weight vector $r\in\mathbb{R}^m$ initialized by $r\leftarrow w$,
(ii) a Boolean mask \texttt{alive}$\in\{0,1\}^m$ indicating which edges are currently kept (initialized to all \texttt{True}), and
(iii) a static adjacency structure \texttt{adj\_e} in which \texttt{adj\_e[u]} stores the indices of all edges with source vertex $u$.
Edges are removed by setting \texttt{alive[e]} to \texttt{False}; the adjacency lists are not rebuilt, and removed edges are skipped via the \texttt{alive} mask.

\paragraph{Cycle detection.}
At each iteration, Phase~1 calls a deterministic DFS-based cycle finder to obtain one directed cycle in the currently alive subgraph.
The cycle finder performs an iterative depth-first search over vertices in increasing index order and explores outgoing edges in the stored adjacency order.
It uses standard three-color marking (\texttt{WHITE}/\texttt{GRAY}/\texttt{BLACK}) together with parent pointers \texttt{parent\_v} and \texttt{parent\_e}.
A back edge to a \texttt{GRAY} vertex identifies a directed cycle, which is reconstructed as a list of edge indices by following parent pointers and returned to the caller.

\paragraph{Local-ratio-style residual reduction and deletion.}
Given a detected cycle $C$ (represented as a list of edge indices), we compute
\[
\Delta \;=\; \min_{e\in C} r_e.
\]
If $\Delta>0$, we subtract $\Delta$ from the residual of every edge on the cycle, i.e., $r_e\leftarrow r_e-\Delta$ for all $e\in C$.
We then delete all edges whose residual is numerically zero, using a tolerance \texttt{zero\_tol} (default $10^{-15}$): edges with $r_e\le\texttt{zero\_tol}$ are removed by setting \texttt{alive[e]} to \texttt{False}.
To guarantee progress in floating-point arithmetic, if residual reduction fails to delete any edge on the current cycle (e.g., due to roundoff), we forcibly delete the minimum-residual edge on the cycle by setting its residual to zero and marking it dead.
This ensures that at least one edge is removed from the alive subgraph per iteration whenever a cycle is found.

\paragraph{Stopping conditions and time budgeting.}
Phase~1 repeats the above loop until either (i) no directed cycle is found in the alive subgraph (in which case the kept subgraph is acyclic), or (ii) a global wall-clock budget \texttt{time\_limit\_sec} is exceeded.
In the latter case Phase~1 terminates early and passes the current kept set to Phase~2; therefore the output of Phase~1 is guaranteed to be acyclic only when it stops due to cycle absence (not due to timeout).
In all experiments, Phase~1 is executed under the same per-run timeout policy as the rest of the non-GNN pipeline (\S\ref{subsec:exp_setup}).

\paragraph{Recorded diagnostics.}
For reproducibility and profiling, the solver records Phase~1 diagnostics in the returned metadata when enabled, including the number of Phase~1 iterations, the number of removed edges after Phase~1, the number of kept edges after Phase~1, and the Phase~1 wall-clock time.
These quantities are used only for analysis and do not affect the ranking output.

\subsection{Phase 2: Stable Weight-Prioritized Add-Back (Variants INS1/INS2/INS3)}
\label{subsec:framework_phase2}

Phase~2 post-processes the acyclic backbone produced by Phase~1 by attempting to reinsert previously removed arcs while preserving acyclicity. The goal of this stage is to recover high-confidence comparisons, as reflected by larger edge weights, without reintroducing directed cycles. To this end, we use a deterministic, time-bounded add-back procedure that scans candidate arcs in stable descending order of weight and accepts an arc only when it is forward with respect to a topological ordering of the current kept subgraph.

\paragraph{Inputs and outputs.}
Phase~2 is applied after Phase~1 and takes as input the vertex count $n$, the full edge list $(\texttt{src},\texttt{dst},\texttt{w})$, and an initial Boolean mask \texttt{kept\_initial} indicating which arcs remain after cycle breaking. It returns a final mask \texttt{kept} describing the post-processed DAG. When enabled, the implementation also records auxiliary information such as the kept mask after each pass, the number of reinserted arcs per pass, and a termination reason.

\paragraph{Stable descending-weight scan.}
Let $w_e$ denote the original weight of arc $e$. We first compute a stable ordering of all arcs in descending order of $w_e$, using a stable sorting procedure so that ties are handled deterministically. Within each pass, arcs are scanned in this fixed order, and only arcs that are currently absent from the kept set are considered for reinsertion. Stable ordering is important because it ensures reproducible tie-breaking among equal-weight arcs and gives priority to stronger comparisons throughout the add-back process.

\paragraph{Acyclicity test within each pass.}
At the beginning of each add-back pass, we compute a topological ordering of the current kept subgraph using Kahn's algorithm. Let $\mathrm{pos}(v)$ denote the position of vertex $v$ in this order. An absent arc $(u,v)$ is accepted if and only if $\mathrm{pos}(u) < \mathrm{pos}(v)$, that is, if the arc is forward with respect to the current topological order. This provides an $O(1)$ acceptance test per arc once the topological positions are known and avoids more expensive dynamic reachability checks during the scan. Importantly, the topological order is computed once at the start of a pass and is not updated after each individual insertion.

\paragraph{Why acyclicity is preserved.}
Because only forward arcs are inserted with respect to a valid topological ordering of the current kept subgraph, that same ordering remains valid after all accepted insertions within the pass. Therefore, the kept subgraph remains acyclic throughout the pass and at its conclusion. If the current kept subgraph is not acyclic, for example because an earlier stage terminated under a time limit before full resolution, Kahn's algorithm will fail to produce a complete topological order; in that case, the implementation stops Phase~2 and records the corresponding termination condition.

\paragraph{Multiple passes and INS variants.}
We consider three multi-pass variants, namely \textsc{OURS-MFAS-INS1}, \textsc{OURS-MFAS-INS2}, and \textsc{OURS-MFAS-INS3}, which execute $P=1,2,3$ add-back passes, respectively. After each pass, a new topological order is computed on the updated kept subgraph, and the same stable descending-weight scan is repeated. Multiple passes are useful because an arc that is backward under one topological order may become forward after other insertions have altered the structure of the kept DAG. In this way, later passes can recover additional high-weight comparisons that were not admissible earlier. Phase~2 terminates early if the global time budget is exhausted, if a pass inserts no new arcs, or if a valid topological order cannot be produced.

\paragraph{Determinism and recorded metadata.}
Phase~2 is deterministic under fixed input and time limits. Determinism follows from the use of stable weight ordering together with a deterministic Kahn queue policy, in which initially eligible zero-indegree vertices are processed in increasing vertex order and newly eligible vertices are appended as they arise. For reproducibility and profiling, the solver records metadata including the number of executed passes, the number of reinserted arcs per pass, and the reason for termination.

\subsection{Ranking Extraction and Order-Preserving Score Refinement}
\label{subsec:framework_phaseC}

After Phases~1--2, the solver maintains a set of kept arcs (encoded by a Boolean mask over the edge list).
This subsection describes (i) how we extract a ranking-consistent score vector from the kept subgraph and (ii) two optional refinement steps that improve upset-style objectives under a fixed time budget.
These steps are used uniformly in the experimental protocol (\S\ref{subsec:exp_setup}) and return a score vector whose induced ordering is evaluated by the same upset metrics as all baselines.

\paragraph{Score extraction from the kept DAG.}
Given a kept mask \texttt{kept}, we compute a topological order of the kept subgraph using Kahn's algorithm (the same routine as in Phase~2).
The queue policy is deterministic: initial zero-indegree vertices are enqueued in increasing vertex id order, and newly eligible vertices are appended as they arise.
Let $\mathrm{pos}(v)$ denote the position of vertex $v$ in the returned topological order (smaller position means earlier in the order).
We assign scores by
\[
s_v \;=\; \max\{1,\; n-\mathrm{pos}(v)\},
\]
so earlier vertices in the topological order receive larger scores (higher rank).
If, due to early termination under a time limit, the kept subgraph is not acyclic and a full topological order cannot be produced, the implementation falls back to the identity order $[1,2,\dots,n]$ for score assignment; this case is recorded via metadata and is not typical under the benchmark time budgets.

\paragraph{Optional local refinement of the induced order (naive-upset swaps).}
Optionally, we apply a bounded local-improvement procedure that directly optimizes the \emph{naive upset} objective while operating on the induced ordering.
Starting from the current order (obtained by sorting scores), the procedure performs repeated sweeps of adjacent swaps.
A swap is accepted if it strictly decreases the weighted naive-upset loss (sum of weights of edges that disagree with the current order) by at least a small numerical tolerance.
The refinement is time-bounded by a local budget and also respects the global per-run time limit; it terminates early if a full sweep makes no improvement.
After refinement, we reconstruct a score vector consistent with the refined order by assigning ranks $n,n-1,\dots,1$ along the order and clamping scores to be at least $1$.
This step can change the induced ranking (permutation), but it preserves the convention that larger scores indicate higher rank.

\paragraph{Optional order-preserving ratio-upset refinement (coordinate-wise ternary search).}
We also implement an order-preserving refinement of \emph{score magnitudes} that targets the ratio-upset objective used in prior work.
Given a fixed ordering induced by the current scores, we optimize score values while enforcing strict monotonicity so that the ordering does not change.
Concretely, we process vertices in nondecreasing score order and update one coordinate at a time using ternary search to minimize the ratio-upset contribution, subject to box constraints
\[
s_{\text{prev}}+\delta \;\le\; s_v \;\le\; s_{\text{next}}-\delta
\]
with a small margin $\delta>0$.
A monotone repair step enforces these constraints after updates.
This refinement is executed for a bounded number of passes and ternary-search iterations per coordinate, and it uses a dedicated budget \texttt{refine\_time\_sec} that is additionally capped by the remaining global time in \texttt{time\_limit\_sec}.
When the remaining budget is below a minimal threshold, the refinement is skipped.

\paragraph{Per-pass scores and metadata.}
When enabled, the solver can record intermediate score vectors after each add-back pass (INS1/INS2/INS3), by computing scores from the kept mask after each Phase~2 pass and optionally applying the same refinement policy to each.
The solver also records timing diagnostics, including the time spent in the refinement stage and whether ratio refinement was enabled; these quantities support profiling and reproducible benchmarking.

\paragraph{Determinism considerations.}
Phases~1--2 and score extraction are deterministic under fixed inputs and time limits (see \S\ref{subsec:framework_problem}--\S\ref{subsec:framework_phase2}).
The naive-swap refinement uses stable sorting for deterministic tie-handling.
The ratio-upset refinement processes vertices in sorted score order using a non-stable sort; therefore, when the current score vector contains exact ties, the internal update order can vary and may lead to minor non-determinism in the refined magnitudes.
This non-determinism affects only score magnitudes and is avoided when scores are strictly ordered by construction.

\subsection{Complexity and Practical Considerations}
\label{subsec:framework_complexity}

We summarize the computational costs of the three stages of the proposed pipeline and relate these costs to the empirical scaling behavior observed in the benchmark. Let $n$ denote the number of vertices, $m$ the number of directed edges with positive weight, $P\in\{1,2,3\}$ the number of add-back passes in the INS variants, and $I$ the number of Phase~1 iterations completed before termination.

\paragraph{Phase~1: cycle breaking.}
Phase~1 maintains a residual weight for each edge together with an \emph{alive} mask indicating which edges remain active. Directed cycles are identified by depth-first search on the currently active subgraph. Since inactive edges are skipped, one cycle-detection call runs in $O(n+m_{\text{alive}})$ time, where $m_{\text{alive}}\le m$. Each iteration reduces residual mass along a detected cycle and deletes all edges whose residual falls below the numerical threshold. Because at least one edge is removed whenever a cycle is processed, the number of iterations is at most $m$ in the worst case. In practice, Phase~1 is also explicitly time-bounded and stops either when no directed cycle remains or when the available time budget is exhausted.

\paragraph{Phase~2: stable weight-prioritized add-back.}
Phase~2 begins with one stable sorting of all edges by descending original weight, which costs $O(m\log m)$. Each add-back pass then computes a topological ordering of the current kept subgraph in $O(n+m)$ time and scans the full edge list once. An absent edge $(u,v)$ is accepted if it is forward with respect to the topological order of that pass, so the acceptance test is $O(1)$ once vertex positions in the topological order are known. Consequently, Phase~2 runs in $O(m\log m + P(n+m))$ time in the worst case, with early termination when no additional edges can be reinserted or when the time budget is reached. By construction, this stage preserves acyclicity throughout each pass.

\paragraph{Phase~3: optional score refinement.}
We implement two optional refinement procedures, both executed under explicit time budgets.

The first is a naive adjacent-swap refinement that improves the weighted upset objective by local swaps in the current order. One sweep examines $O(n)$ adjacent pairs, and evaluating each candidate swap requires scanning the edge set, giving worst-case cost $O(nm)$ per sweep. In practice, however, this stage is run with a small number of sweeps and a strict local time budget.

The second is an order-preserving ratio refinement that adjusts score magnitudes without changing the induced ordering. This refinement constructs arrays over unordered vertex pairs $(i,j)$ with nonzero total weight $A_{ij}+A_{ji}$; in the worst case, this structure has size $\Theta(n^2)$. It then performs coordinate-wise updates using ternary search while enforcing strict monotonicity constraints. This stage is likewise explicitly budgeted and is capped by the remaining global time.

\paragraph{Memory footprint.}
The dominant memory cost comes from the sparse edge-list representation: endpoint arrays, weights, residuals, and masks all require $O(m)$ storage, and the adjacency lists also store $O(m)$ edge references. Phase~2 adds one sorted index array of length $m$. If ratio refinement is enabled, the auxiliary pairwise structures can require up to $O(n^2)$ memory in the worst case. The pipeline is otherwise designed for sparse inputs and does not require densifying the adjacency matrix.

\paragraph{Practical scaling and the Finance bottleneck.}
All stages operate under a single wall-clock budget, and the experimental driver enforces a non-GNN timeout of $1800$\,s (see \S\ref{subsec:exp_setup}). The main scalability limitation in the current benchmark arises on very large or dense graphs, most notably Finance with $m\approx 1.7\times 10^6$. In such cases, the combination of repeated cycle searches in Phase~1, full-edge scans in each add-back pass, and potentially quadratic pairwise structures in the ratio-refinement stage can exhaust the available time budget. This behavior is consistent with the intended operating regime of the method: the pipeline is effective on large sparse and moderately dense comparison graphs, while extremely dense instances place simultaneous pressure on the iterative cycle-breaking stage and on the global edge- and pair-based refinement procedures.

\paragraph{Evaluation metrics.}
We report three upset-based losses computed from the weighted adjacency matrix $A$ and the predicted score vector $s$, where larger scores indicate higher rank. Let $M=A-A^\top$, and define score differences $T_{ij}=s_i-s_j$ over pairs with $M_{ij}\neq 0$. \emph{Upset-naive} is the fraction of pairs for which the predicted sign disagrees with $\mathrm{sign}(M_{ij})$. \emph{Upset-simple} averages the squared disagreement between $\mathrm{sign}(T_{ij})$ and $\mathrm{sign}(M_{ij})$. \emph{Upset-ratio} normalizes both score differences and pairwise edge imbalance by their corresponding sums and averages the resulting squared deviation:
\[
T^{(r)}_{ij}=\frac{s_i-s_j}{s_i+s_j+\varepsilon},
\qquad
M^{(r)}_{ij}=\frac{A_{ij}-A_{ji}}{A_{ij}+A_{ji}+\varepsilon},
\]
where $\varepsilon>0$ is a small constant.

\paragraph{Protocol and compute.}
All methods are evaluated with \texttt{TRIALS=10} repeated runs for each dataset--method pair. For all real datasets in the suite, the dataset seed is fixed internally to \texttt{seeds=[10]}. As a result, deterministic non-GNN methods produce identical outputs across trials apart from timing variability and timeout effects, whereas GNN methods can vary across runs because of stochastic training. Experiments are executed on a SLURM cluster using a typical allocation of one task, $48$ CPU cores, and $100$~GB memory. Non-GNN methods, including classical baselines and the proposed variants, are run under a per-run wall-clock budget of $1800$\,s, while GNN methods are run under a $7200$\,s budget. Timed-out runs are recorded as missing values and are excluded from aggregate statistics via NaN-robust averaging.

In addition to these native budgets, we also report a compute-matched view in which all families are restricted to runs that finish within $1800$\,s. Coverage is reported explicitly in that view. Under the recorded protocol, the compute-matched dataset set is a strict subset of the full suite because the ERO-style instance does not complete within $1800$\,s.

\paragraph{Unified execution and evaluation.}
All non-GNN methods, including the classical baselines and the proposed variants, are evaluated through the same non-neural benchmark pathway. Each method produces a score vector $s\in\mathbb{R}^n$, where larger values indicate higher rank. When a method naturally returns only an ordering, we convert that ordering to deterministic rank-based scores so that all methods can be evaluated under the same three upset losses defined in \S\ref{subsec:exp_setup}. Learning-based methods are trained and evaluated separately through the GNNRank framework, but their outputs are scored using the same loss implementations for consistency. Because the dataset seed is fixed for all real datasets (Table~\ref{tab:protocol_summary}), non-GNN methods are deterministic under fixed time limits, whereas GNN runs may vary because of stochastic optimization.

\paragraph{Classical ranking baselines.}
Our classical benchmark suite includes ten standard training-free ranking methods: \textsc{SpringRank}, \textsc{SyncRank}, \textsc{SerialRank}, \textsc{BTL}, \textsc{DavidScore}, \textsc{EigenvectorCentrality}, \textsc{PageRank}, \textsc{RankCentrality}, \textsc{SVD-RS}, and \textsc{SVD-NRS}. These methods map the comparison graph directly to a score vector and span statistical, spectral, centrality-based, and physics-inspired ranking families. We use the default settings provided by the corresponding public implementations and do not tune hyperparameters on a per-dataset basis.

\paragraph{Proposed pipeline and variants.}
The proposed method is a combinatorial, training-free pipeline that first constructs an acyclic comparison backbone and then optionally densifies that backbone while preserving acyclicity. Concretely, it (i) removes arcs to eliminate directed cycles using local-ratio-style residual reduction, (ii) reinserts previously removed arcs in stable descending weight order subject to acyclicity, and (iii) extracts a score vector from the resulting DAG and optionally refines score magnitudes under the same upset-based objectives used for evaluation (see \S\ref{sec:framework}). We report four non-GNN variants: \textsc{OURS-MFAS}, \textsc{OURS-MFAS-INS1}, \textsc{OURS-MFAS-INS2}, and \textsc{OURS-MFAS-INS3}. These variants differ only in the number of add-back passes, with INS1, INS2, and INS3 corresponding to $P=1,2,3$, respectively. Unless stated otherwise, we report \textsc{OURS-MFAS-INS3} as \textbf{OURS} in the headline summaries, while INS1 and INS2 serve as lighter variants of the same pipeline.

\paragraph{Complexity and time-bounded operation.}
Let $n$ and $m$ denote the number of vertices and directed edges in the input graph, and let $P\in\{1,2,3\}$ denote the number of add-back passes. Phase~2 sorts edges once by weight, costing $O(m\log m)$, and then performs up to $P$ passes. Each pass computes a topological ordering of the currently kept graph and scans edges in weight order with $O(1)$ acceptance checks, yielding worst-case complexity $O(m\log m + P(n+m))$. Phase~1 repeatedly detects and breaks directed cycles; each cycle-finding call runs in $O(n+m_{\text{alive}})$ over the currently active edges, and at least one edge is deleted whenever a cycle is found, so the number of iterations is at most $m$ in the worst case. In practice, all phases operate under a fixed wall-clock budget (Table~\ref{tab:protocol_summary}), so our emphasis is on empirical runtime under shared budgets rather than on introducing a new approximation guarantee.

\paragraph{GNNRank methods and configurations.}
To represent learning-based alternatives, we include two directed-GNN backbones from the GNNRank framework: \textsc{DIGRAC} and \textsc{ib}. For each backbone, we report two fixed benchmark configurations: a distance-based configuration and a proximal-baseline configuration with distance-based pretraining. This yields four GNN configurations per dataset in total. In the main tables, these configurations are reported explicitly. When we provide compact ``best-of'' comparisons against GNNRank, we treat them only as oracle envelopes over this fixed candidate set and not as standalone methods.

\paragraph{Timeout handling and missingness.}
The benchmark enforces per-run wall-clock limits of $1800$\,s for non-GNN methods and $7200$\,s for GNN methods (Table~\ref{tab:protocol_summary}). Timed-out runs are recorded as missing values and are excluded from aggregate statistics via NaN-robust averaging. Coverage, defined as the number of datasets contributing to a method’s summary statistics, is reported explicitly throughout the results section. Under the $1800$\,s non-GNN budget, all proposed variants time out on the Finance instance, so \textbf{OURS} contributes metrics on $77$ of the $80$ datasets in the full suite. Any remaining missing values, including missing runtime entries or explicit dataset-level exclusions, are reported in the missingness audit rather than omitted silently.

\paragraph{Aggregation and numerical precision.}
To reduce artifacts from log formatting or rounding, our primary table construction uses full-precision per-trial arrays saved from the benchmark runs whenever these are available. If a dataset--method entry is unavailable in the saved arrays, we fall back to the corresponding per-dataset summary extracted from experiment logs and record that fallback in the audit outputs. All tables and figures in \S\ref{subsec:main_results}--\S\ref{subsec:exp_contribution} are generated from the same per-method CSV artifacts, and an automated validation step is used to check internal consistency of coverage and missingness summaries.

\subsection{Contribution and Practical Advantages}
\label{subsec:exp_contribution}

This subsection summarizes the practical contribution of the proposed method as a scalable, training-free ranking pipeline under fixed wall-clock budgets. The method constructs a ranking directly from the comparison graph by enforcing acyclicity, selectively restoring high-weight edges while preserving that acyclicity, and then extracting scores consistent with the recovered structure. Accordingly, we do \emph{not} present the method as a new state-of-the-art algorithm for minimum weighted feedback arc set in the sense of new approximation guarantees. Rather, the contribution is a deterministic and deployable ranking pipeline grounded in the MWFAS viewpoint and evaluated under the same upset-based criteria used in recent ranking benchmarks.

Two empirical messages emerge from the benchmark. First, \textsc{OURS-MFAS-INS3} (denoted \textbf{OURS}) is frequently competitive with strong classical ranking baselines on a per-dataset basis. Second, relative to training-based GNNRank configurations, \textbf{OURS} often matches or improves upset losses while providing a clear runtime advantage because it avoids training. Taken together, these findings support the paper’s main practical claim: combinatorial acyclicity-based ranking can offer a favorable accuracy--runtime trade-off on large pairwise-comparison graphs.

\paragraph{Competitiveness relative to classical baselines.}
To place \textbf{OURS} relative to the strongest behavior observed among the classical methods, we compare it against a \emph{best-in-suite} classical reference defined post hoc on each dataset as the minimum upset loss among the ten reported classical baselines. This reference is used only as context and is not treated as a standalone algorithm. The comparison is computed on the $77$ datasets for which \textbf{OURS} returns a valid value under the non-GNN $1800$\,s budget.

Under upset-simple, \textbf{OURS} records $38$ wins and $39$ losses against the best-in-suite classical reference, with median gap $0.0244$ and median relative gap $0.0303$. It lies within $10\%$ of the classical reference on $42/77$ datasets. Under upset-ratio, \textbf{OURS} records $45$ wins and $32$ losses, with median relative gap $-0.0781$, and lies within $10\%$ of the classical reference on $47/77$ datasets. These results indicate that the proposed pipeline is often close to the strongest classical performance observed on a dataset, even though it is not tuned on a per-instance basis.

\paragraph{Accuracy relative to training-based GNNRank configurations.}
We also compare \textbf{OURS} against a \emph{best-in-suite} GNN reference defined post hoc on each dataset as the minimum upset loss among the fixed set of reported GNN configurations. As above, this serves only as compact context and does not replace the per-configuration reporting in the main tables.

Across the same $77$ datasets for which both \textbf{OURS} and the GNN reference are defined, \textbf{OURS} records $45$ wins, $1$ tie, and $31$ losses under upset-simple, with median gap $-0.1822$ and median relative gap $-0.1701$. It is within $10\%$ of the GNN reference on $48/77$ datasets. Under upset-ratio, \textbf{OURS} records $47$ wins and $30$ losses, with median relative gap $-0.2282$, and is within $10\%$ of the GNN reference on $47/77$ datasets. Overall, these comparisons show that a training-free ranking pipeline can remain competitive even against stronger but substantially more expensive learning-based alternatives.

\paragraph{Runtime--accuracy trade-off and deployment advantages.}
The main operational advantage of \textbf{OURS} is that it avoids training and runs under the same non-GNN wall-clock budget as the classical baselines. To quantify this advantage, we compare \textbf{OURS} with the best-in-suite GNN configuration on each dataset for which both runtimes are recorded. On these $76$ datasets, define the speedup
\[
S \;=\; \frac{t_{\text{GNN}}}{t_{\text{OURS}}}\,,
\]
where $t_{\text{GNN}}$ is the runtime of the selected GNN reference and $t_{\text{OURS}}$ is the runtime of \textbf{OURS}.

The resulting speedups are strongly favorable to \textbf{OURS}: the $25$th percentile is $4.72\times$, the median is $10.16\times$, and the $75$th percentile is $18.18\times$, with mean $62.69\times$ due to a heavy tail of very slow GNN runs. Moreover, \textbf{OURS} achieves at least $10\times$ speedup on $38/76$ datasets, at least $50\times$ on $17/76$, and at least $100\times$ on $13/76$ datasets. Using upset-simple as the accuracy criterion, the Pareto breakdown over these $76$ datasets is especially informative: \textbf{OURS} is \emph{better and faster} on $45$ datasets and \emph{worse but faster} on $31$ datasets, and it is never slower than the selected GNN configuration under the recorded protocol. This makes the deployment trade-off clear: the proposed method provides deterministic ranking with substantial runtime savings while still improving accuracy on a substantial fraction of instances.

\paragraph{Coverage and limitations.}
The method also has clear limitations. Under the $1800$\,s non-GNN budget, \textbf{OURS} does not complete on the largest extremely dense instances, most notably Finance, and the comparisons above are therefore reported only on datasets for which valid outputs are available. In addition, the dominant accuracy tail relative to both classical and GNN references is concentrated in the higher-resolution \emph{finer basketball} instances. These behaviors are consistent with the design of the pipeline: Phase~1 repeatedly searches for cycles and removes arcs, while Phase~2 performs full edge scans in each pass, and both costs become more demanding as graph density increases. At the same time, finer temporal resolution can amplify local inconsistencies that are difficult to resolve using conservative acyclicity-preserving reinsertion alone. These limitations motivate future improvements such as strongly-connected-component-local refinement, reachability-aware insertion rules, and score-refinement procedures that avoid quadratic pair constructions on dense graphs.
\section{Conclusion and Future Research}
This paper presents a scalable, training-free ranking pipeline for weighted pairwise-comparison graphs based on the connection between ranking inconsistency and the Minimum Weighted Feedback Arc Set (MWFAS) formulation. The proposed method combines a local-ratio-style cycle-breaking stage to construct an acyclic backbone with a stable weight-prioritized add-back procedure (INS1/INS2/INS3 variants) that restores high-weight comparisons while preserving acyclicity. From the resulting graph, the pipeline produces real-valued scores whose induced order is evaluated under the same upset-based losses used in recent ranking benchmarks.

Across a benchmark suite of $80$ instances under fixed wall-clock budgets, the proposed method is competitive with strong classical baselines, including \textsc{BTL}, spectral methods, and centrality-based methods, while also providing a consistent runtime advantage over training-based GNNRank configurations on instances where both sides are recorded. These results support the broader practical message of the paper: combinatorial acyclicity-based structure can serve as an effective, deterministic, and deployable alternative for ranking from pairwise comparisons when training cost, reproducibility, and runtime efficiency are important.

Several directions remain for future research. First, improved add-back and refinement strategies, such as reachability-aware insertion or targeted local search within strongly connected components, may reduce the remaining gap on the higher-resolution ``finer'' instances while preserving the time-bounded design. Second, better scalability on extremely dense graphs will likely require faster cycle detection, incremental maintenance of acyclicity constraints, and refinement procedures that avoid quadratic pair constructions. Third, it would be valuable to study principled tie-handling and uncertainty quantification when the comparison graph admits many valid topological orders or contains weakly connected components. Finally, an important direction is to design ranking objectives and calibration procedures that align more closely with downstream upset-based evaluation while retaining the computational advantages of acyclicity-based ranking.
